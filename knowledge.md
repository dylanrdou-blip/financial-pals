## Savings account basics
A savings account is a bank account that holds money you don't plan to spend right away and pays you interest. The money is usually easy to take out, and deposits at banks and credit unions in the US are typically insured up to a limit. It's a good home for an emergency fund or short-term goals like a trip or a laptop.

## Checking vs savings accounts
A checking account is for everyday spending: debit card, bill pay, and direct deposit. A savings account is for money you want to keep and grow. Many people keep both and move a set amount into savings each payday so saving happens automatically. Check for monthly fees and look for accounts that waive them for students.

## Emergency fund
An emergency fund is money set aside for surprises like a car repair, a medical bill, or a lost job. A common goal is three to six months of basic living costs, but starting with even one week of expenses is a real win. Keep it in a savings account that's easy to reach, not in the stock market, because you may need it at a bad time.

## Budgeting basics
A budget is a plan for your money: what comes in, what goes out, and what's left. One popular starting rule is 50/30/20, with about half of income for needs, 30 percent for wants, and 20 percent for savings and debt payoff. It's only a guide, and students on tight incomes often adjust the percentages. Track your spending for a month first so you know where your money actually goes.

## APR vs APY
APR is the yearly interest rate you pay on borrowing, like a credit card or loan. APY is the yearly rate you earn on savings, and it includes compounding. When borrowing, a lower APR is better. When saving, a higher APY is better.

## Compound interest
Compound interest means you earn interest on your original money plus the interest it has already earned, so growth speeds up over time. The earlier you start saving or investing, the more time compounding has to work, even if you start with small amounts. It works against you with debt too, because unpaid interest can grow the balance.

## Credit cards and building credit
A credit card lets you borrow money for purchases and pay it back later. If you pay the full statement balance each month, you generally pay no interest. Carrying a balance can get expensive because credit card APRs are usually high. A common way to build credit is to make small purchases, pay on time every month, and keep your balance well below your limit.

## Credit score basics
A credit score is a number lenders use to judge how risky it is to lend to you. The main factors are paying on time, how much of your credit limit you use, how long you've had credit, how often you apply for new credit, and the mix of accounts you have. Paying on time and keeping your usage low matter most. You can check your credit reports for free through the official site AnnualCreditReport.com.

## Student loans
Federal student loans are issued by the government and usually come with repayment options and protections that private loans often lack. Private loans come from banks and other lenders and have their own terms. If you borrow, take federal loans first, borrow only what you need, and know that interest may build up while you're in school depending on the loan type. Rules change, so check studentaid.gov for current details.

## Paying off debt
Two popular methods are the avalanche and the snowball. With the avalanche, you make minimum payments on everything and put extra money toward the debt with the highest interest rate, which saves the most money. With the snowball, you put extra money toward the smallest balance first, which gives quick wins that help some people stay motivated. Pick the one you will actually stick with.

## Retirement accounts
Retirement accounts like a 401(k) or an IRA are built for long-term saving and often come with tax benefits. A 401(k) is offered through an employer, and some employers match part of what you contribute, which is extra money for you. An IRA is one you open yourself. A Roth account is funded with money you've already paid tax on, while a traditional account usually gives a tax break now and is taxed later. If you're a student with a small income, starting early with small amounts still helps.

## How to think about investing
Investing means putting money into things like stocks and funds that can grow over time but can also lose value. Money you need within a few years usually shouldn't be invested, because prices can drop. Many beginners use low-cost index funds, which spread your money across many companies instead of betting on one. Nobody can reliably predict which stocks will do best in a given quarter, so focus on time, diversification, and low fees instead of tips.

## Avoiding scams
Scammers often pretend to be banks, schools, employers, or the government, and they push you to act fast. Be suspicious of anyone who asks for gift cards, wire transfers, or your passwords, or who promises guaranteed returns or easy money. If a message feels urgent, stop and contact the company directly using a phone number or website you already trust. Never share verification codes with anyone.
